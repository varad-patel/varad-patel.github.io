Game of Life Emergence Meter

Draw cells, press run, and watch the meters: how much disorder the density allows, how much the pattern really has, and how much of what is new is organised. Then ask whether any of that is what people mean by emergence.

One finding

A 96 by 64 soup with half its cells alive (seed 25) starts as noise: 1.000 bits of entropy per cell and 0.000 of structure. One generation of Life's rule cuts the density to 26.4% and creates 0.015 bits per cell of structure. Structure peaks at 0.070 at generation 48, and complexity, a measure made for this page and not a standard one, at 0.079 at generation 160. At generation 1,770 the grid starts to repeat with 157 cells left: structure is still 0.030 and 0.9% of cells still flip every generation, but complexity is exactly zero. On the same grid, noise redrawn every generation stayed below 0.002 on both meters for 8,000 generations.

A phone opens this page on a smaller grid, 48 by 32, where the same seed settles at generation 188 with 60 cells. Choose "96 by 64 cells" under Grid to see the run described here.

The idea

John Conway's Game of Life has one rule, and it fits in a sentence: a cell is alive in the next generation if exactly three of its eight neighbours are alive now, or if it is alive now and exactly two are. Martin Gardner introduced it to the public in October 1970. The glider, five cells that walk across the grid, was already known. Before the year was out Bill Gosper's group at MIT had found a gun that fires gliders for ever, and Berlekamp, Conway and Guy later showed that the rule can carry out any computation at all. None of these things is mentioned in the rule.

That gap is what the word emergence points at, and Life is the cleanest place to argue about it because nothing is hidden. A reductionist says, correctly, that every cell at every moment is fixed by the rule and the starting grid, so there is nothing over and above the cells. The reply grants that and adds two things. First, the glider is a pattern with regularities of its own ("it moves one cell diagonally every four generations") that let you predict the grid with far less work than updating cells. Daniel Dennett used Life to argue that such patterns are real for exactly that reason. Second, for many questions, such as whether a given soup ever settles, nobody knows a shortcut: the only way to find out is to run it. Mark Bedau called this weak emergence: the large-scale behaviour follows from the rule, but can be derived only by simulating it. Philip Anderson's slogan for the general point was "more is different". What Life does not show is strong emergence, in which, on David Chalmers's definition, truths about the higher level could not be deduced even in principle from the lower one. Here the cells and the rule fix everything, by construction.

The argument is not only about toy grids. When Hayek described prices as coordinating knowledge that no single mind holds, and when Schelling showed that mild individual preferences can produce sharply segregated cities, they were making claims of the same shape: an order that nobody put in, which is better described at its own level than at the level of its parts. Whether such descriptions explain anything, or merely summarise, is a live question in economics, in politics and in the philosophy of mind.

This page tries the obvious empirical move: put a number on "order that was not put in" and watch it. The meters do show the rule manufacturing pattern out of noise. Whether that is a measurement of emergence is a separate question, and the answer given below is mostly no.

How it works

The grid and the rule

The grid has N cells, each 0 or 1, and wraps at its edges, so every cell has exactly eight neighbours and a glider that leaves on the right comes back on the left. Each generation the tool counts the live neighbours of every cell in the current grid and writes the result into a second array: alive if the count is 3, or if the count is 2 and the cell is alive already. The arrays are typed arrays of bytes and are reused, so nothing is allocated while it runs.

Density and the entropy it allows

The density ρ is the share of cells that are alive. If that were all you knew, and cells were independent of one another, describing the grid would cost

H1 = −ρ log2 ρ − (1 − ρ) log2(1 − ρ)

bits per cell. This is Shannon's entropy of a biased coin. It is 1 bit when half the cells are alive and 0 when the grid is empty or full. It says how much disorder the density leaves room for, not how much there is.

Entropy in 2 by 2 blocks

To see how much disorder there is, the tool looks at cells four at a time. Every cell is taken once as the top-left corner of a 2 by 2 block, wrapping at the edges, which gives N overlapping blocks. Each block is one of 16 patterns. With pb the share of blocks showing pattern b,

H4 = −Σb pb log2 pb   bits per block
h2 = H4 / 4   bits per cell

Structure

S = H1 − h2

Because of the wrap, each of the four positions in a block is alive in exactly a share ρ of blocks, so independent cells would give H4 = 4H1. The entropy of a whole is never more than the sum of the entropies of its parts, and equals it only when the parts are independent. So S is never negative, and it is zero only when the four cells of a block are independent of one another. 4S is what Watanabe called the total correlation of the block: the bits saved by describing four neighbours together instead of one by one. "Structure" is this page's name for it. A checkerboard scores 0.75, because half its cells are alive yet only two of the sixteen blocks ever occur, and nothing can score more: H4 is never less than the entropy of one of its four cells, so S is at most three quarters of H1.

Activity and complexity

Activity is the share of cells that changed in the last generation. It is high for noise and for a field of blinkers alike, so it is not a measure of anything interesting.

The complexity meter is this page's own construction. It is not a measure from the literature and has not been checked against one. The tool builds a novelty map: cell i is marked if its state differs from its state two generations ago. Complexity is the structure measure again, applied to the novelty map in place of the grid:

nt(i) = xt(i) ⊕ xt−2(i)
ν = share of cells marked in nt
C = H1(ν) − H4(nt) / 4

Here ⊕ is "exclusive or" (1 when the two states differ), H1(ν) is the coin entropy above with ν in place of ρ, and H4(nt) is the block entropy above counted on the novelty map. C is in bits per cell and is undefined for the first two generations. It asks how spatially organised the new things are, and it was built to pass three checks. An empty grid has no novelty, so C = 0. A still life has none either, and nor does anything that repeats every two generations, which covers the blinkers that make up nearly all the moving debris Life leaves behind; that is the reason for looking two generations back and not one. Pure noise has plenty of novelty, but each cell's novelty is independent of its neighbours', so the gap closes and C is zero again. What scores above zero is change that hangs together in space: a glider, or the edge of a region that has not yet settled.

What it cannot distinguish follows from the definition. It sees only 2 by 2 windows of the novelty map, so a glider, a spaceship and the ragged edge of a dying blob are the same thing to it: so many cells of organised change. It reads above zero for ever for anything that repeats with a period of three or more, such as a pulsar, or a glider going round the wrapped grid, so it does not tell repetition from novelty. And because it is an average over the grid, one object reads lower the larger the grid: a single glider reads about three times lower on a grid with four times the cells.

The noise figures are not exactly zero because entropies estimated from a finite grid are biased slightly downwards, and by different amounts for single cells and for blocks. The bias shrinks as the grid grows: noise averages about 0.0003 on both meters at 96 by 64 and about 0.0013 at 48 by 32, the grid a phone starts on, where about one generation in four reads 0.002 or more on one meter or the other. Readings that small mean nothing on the small grid.

The two controls for checking the meters

"Shuffle cells" deals the live cells out to random positions. The density, and so H1, is untouched; the structure collapses to about zero. Setting the rule to "Noise" redraws every cell at random each generation, at the density the grid had when you switched. Both let you see for yourself what the meters read when there is nothing to find.

Settling

After every generation the tool compares the grid with the grid two generations earlier. When they are equal, the pattern is a still life or a period-2 cycle and will never do anything new, so the tool says so and pauses. That is also the moment complexity reaches exactly zero. Random soups are reproducible: the seed and the density fix every cell. "Restart" goes back to generation 0, with anything that was drawn on the grid before it ran.

The grid on a small screen

A finger that starts on the grid draws and does not scroll the page, so the grid is never allowed more than six tenths of the screen's height, which leaves room to scroll from above or below it. Narrow or short screens start on 48 by 32 cells so that a cell is big enough to touch. Every number quoted on this page is for 96 by 64 unless it says otherwise. "Show the grid as text" under the meters gives the same grid as rows of characters.

What this cannot tell you

  • Whether anything has emerged. Claims about emergence are claims about levels of description: that "glider" names something real, that the glider level predicts or explains in a way the cell level does not. The meters are statistics of four neighbouring cells. They register that the rule turns noise into pattern, and they are silent on whether that pattern is a thing, a convenient summary or only a way of talking. A number going up is not an argument for either side.
  • Order from organisation. Structure is highest for a checkerboard, which nobody finds interesting. A crystal and an organism both depart from randomness. The complexity meter is zero for anything with period 1 or 2, but a period-3 oscillator, or a single glider circling the wrapped grid for ever, scores above zero, so it cannot tell endless repetition from open-ended novelty.
  • Anything larger than four cells. The glider gun is 36 cells wide and its function, emitting gliders, is invisible to a 2 by 2 window. A glider and a shapeless dying blob can score the same. Objects, long-range order and anything resembling computation are out of sight.
  • How remarkable a pattern is. Every meter is an average per cell, so it measures how much structure there is across the grid, not how organised any one object is. A single glider on a large grid reads almost nothing, and it is about as organised as five cells can be.
  • What Life does on an unbounded plane. The edges wrap. On the 96 by 64 grid the gun's gliders come round and hit it by generation 780, and the R-pentomino runs into its own debris and gliders: it settles at generation 1,123 with 142 cells, not at 1,103 with 116 as it does when it has room.
  • That this is the measure of complexity. There is no agreed one, and the complexity meter here is not any of the published ones: it was made for this page. Crutchfield's statistical complexity, Bennett's logical depth and the López-Ruiz, Mancini and Calbet measure are different quantities with different aims, and Feldman and Crutchfield argued that the last of these is not a general measure of structure. This page's measure was designed to pass three sanity checks and has been tested against nothing else. The choice of window, of the two-generation lag and of binary states are all the observer's, which is itself a point in the debate: what counts as pattern depends on the description you bring.

Improvements and extensions

  • Measure blocks of growing size and track how the entropy per cell converges, which gives the excess entropy: structure at every scale the grid can show, not only between neighbours.
  • Test Dennett's claim directly. Take a census of objects (still lifes, blinkers, gliders) and compare the length of the object-level description with the cell-level one, generation by generation.
  • Add local information measures such as transfer entropy, which Lizier, Prokopenko and Zomaya showed pick out the gliders of one-dimensional automata as the places where information moves.
  • Offer other rules and plot the meters against Langton's λ parameter, to see whether structure and complexity really peak between the frozen rules and the chaotic ones.
  • Replace the wrapped grid with an unbounded one so that guns and methuselahs behave as they do in the literature.
  • Estimate the noise floor for the current grid size and density by simulation and draw it on the charts, so that small readings can be told from zero.
