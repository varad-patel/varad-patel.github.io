Ship of Theseus Simulator

Replace the parts of a ship one at a time, mark the step at which you think it stops being the same ship, and see which view of identity your mark agrees with.

One finding

On this 46-part galley, with the keel counted as 20 planks, half of the original weight has gone after 6 replacements if the keel and frame go first, and not until the 41st if the worn parts go first. A head count of parts says step 23 either way. Raise the keel's weight to 120 planks, so that it alone is more than half the ship, and even the view that gradual change preserves identity finds a break: the one step that replaces the keel.

The idea

Plutarch reports that the Athenians kept the thirty-oared ship in which Theseus came back from Crete, and preserved it for centuries by taking out each timber as it decayed and fitting a sound one in its place. The ship, he says, became a standing example for philosophers arguing about growth and change: some held that it remained the same ship, others that it did not.

In 1655 Thomas Hobbes made the puzzle sharper. Suppose someone had kept every old plank as it came out, and afterwards put them all together again in the same order. Now there are two ships. One has an unbroken history as the ship of Theseus and none of his timber. The other has all of his timber and a gap in its history. If sameness of form and history is what makes a ship the same, the first is the original; if sameness of matter is, the second; and they cannot both be, since they are two ships.

The question is what it takes for anything to last through change, and it is not only about boats. A living body replaces its matter. A university, a football club or a company can lose every founding member and carry on. A shrine can be rebuilt on a fixed cycle and remain the same shrine to those who use it. A state after a revolution may or may not owe the old state's debts. Each of these is settled, in practice, by choosing which kind of sameness counts.

The main answers fall into three families. Identity follows the matter: at the limit, mereological essentialism, on which a thing cannot lose a single part and remain strictly the same. Identity follows form and a continuous history: the line that runs from Aristotle's distinction between a thing's matter and its form to modern accounts of continuity under a kind. Or the question is posed wrongly: on the four-dimensional view a ship is extended in time as it is in space, every stage is a part of it, and what looked like a question about survival becomes a question about which stages we group under one name. This page lets you find your own breaking point and then shows which family it belongs to.

How it works

The ship

The galley has N = 46 parts: fifteen oars (the bank on the side you can see), a sail, a steering oar, a yard, twenty-four hull planks, a mast, a stem post, a stern post and a keel. Each step replaces exactly one part with a new part of the same shape in the same place. Three orders are built in: worn parts first with the keel last, the reverse, and a seeded random order. Clicking a part replaces that part next, which makes the order your own.

Two measures of what is left

After t replacements the share of parts that are original is

c(t) = (N − t) / N

That treats an oar and the keel alike. The second measure gives part i a weight wi and reports the share of weight that is original:

m(t) = 1 − (sum of wi over replaced parts) / (sum of wi over all parts)

wi = biγ

The base importance b is 20 for the keel, 8 for the mast, 5 for each post and the sail, 2 for the yard and the steering oar, 1 for a plank and 0.5 for an oar. These are a judgement, not a measurement. The slider sets γ from 0 to 2. At 0 every part weighs the same and the two measures coincide; at 1 the base values are used as they are; at 2 the keel counts as 400 planks. A third quantity is the size of a single step, the share of the whole ship that one replacement changes:

s(t) = wpart replaced at t / (sum of wi over all parts)

Where each view puts the break

  • Mereological essentialism: step 1. Any change of parts is a change of thing.
  • Majority of original parts: the first t with c(t) ≤ ½, which is step 23.
  • Majority of original matter: the first t with m(t) ≤ ½. This depends on the order and on the weights.
  • An essential core: the step that replaces the keel.
  • Gradual continuity: the first t with s(t) ≥ ½, and otherwise never. Each stage must overlap with the stage before by more than half.
  • Same form and function: never. Every replacement is like for like, so the design, the arrangement and the ability to sail are the same at every step.
  • Four-dimensionalism: no step. The ship is the whole sequence of stages.

Matching your mark

A mark of "never" agrees with form and function, and with gradual continuity when no step reaches half the ship. Three views are tied to a single event, and a mark agrees with them only if it falls on that very step: the first replacement for essentialism, the keel for the essential core, a change of half the ship for the break that gradual continuity can find. The two majority views are matched when their break lies within two replacements of the mark; that allowance is a convenience, not a principle. A mark that agrees with none of these is reported as a threshold of your own, with the two shares that remained at that step.

Hobbes's variant

With the variant switched on, each part taken off the first ship is fixed in the same position on a second. After t steps the second ship is 1 − m(t) complete by weight, and every part of it is original. The tool asks which ship is the ship of Theseus and checks the answer against your mark: you cannot hold both that the repaired ship stopped being the ship at some step and that the repaired ship is the ship.

What this cannot tell you

  • Which view is true. The tool sorts marks by the view they agree with. Counting planks does not settle a question in metaphysics, and it may be that the question has no answer beyond what a museum, an insurer or a navy chooses to count.
  • Where a threshold belongs. "More than half" is a convention. No argument picks out 50% over 40% or 60%, and that arbitrariness is a standard objection to every view that draws a line through gradual change.
  • What the weights should be. Whether a keel matters twenty times as much as a plank is your input. Weight could mean mass, cost, structural role or attachment, and each gives a different curve.
  • What the full theories say. Each view is one rule here and a literature elsewhere. Chisholm's essentialist grants that the ship persists in a loose and popular sense. Wiggins's continuity is continuity under a kind, not a 50% overlap test. Four-dimensionalists disagree among themselves about whether a ship is the whole worm or a single stage.
  • Cases outside the model. Every change here is like for like and one part at a time. There is no change of design or use, no long spell in pieces, no difference between a refit in an afternoon and one over a century, though all of these move people's judgements.
  • Anything about persons. What matters in a person's survival may be memory and character, not matter or shape, and Derek Parfit argued that identity is not what matters at all. A ship has no point of view to lose.

Improvements and extensions

  • Let the visitor build a view by mixing matter, continuity and form with their own weights, in the manner of Nozick's closest-continuer theory, and show when the answer to Hobbes flips.
  • Add changes that are not like for like: a steel plank, an engine in place of the oars, a ship moved into a museum.
  • Add time: the same replacements in a day or over three centuries, and a period in which the old parts lie in a warehouse.
  • Swap the ship for other things with parts: a body and its cells, a band and its members, a state and its institutions.
  • Ask for two marks, "certainly still the ship" and "certainly not", and treat the gap between them as day 15 treats the heap.
  • Collect marks from many visitors and show their distribution, which would need a server; at present nothing leaves the browser.
