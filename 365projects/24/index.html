Personal Forecast Log

Write down what you think will happen and how sure you are. Come back when the day arrives, say what happened, and let the arithmetic tell you how good your sense of "probably" is.

One finding

A forecaster who says 90% and is right 70% of the time earns a Brier score of exactly 0.25, the same as someone who answers 50% to everything. The example log shows how hard it is to do much better: across its 24 resolved predictions the forecaster averaged 80% confidence and was right 71% of the time, which scores 0.216, only 14% better than answering 50% every time. With so few forecasts, even that 9-point gap is within what luck alone would produce.

The idea

Everyone forecasts and almost nobody keeps score. "Probably", "very likely" and "I'd be surprised" cost nothing to say and cannot be shown wrong afterwards. A number can. One forecast of 70% proves little whichever way it goes, but a hundred of them make a testable claim: about seventy of those things should happen. If fifty-five do, the forecaster's 70% does not mean what it says.

Glenn Brier, a meteorologist, proposed in 1950 a way to turn that into a single figure: score each forecast by the squared distance between the probability and what happened. The rule has a property that matters more than its simplicity. It is proper: the way to get the best score you can expect is to report exactly what you believe. Rounding your 70% up to 90% to sound decisive, or down to 55% to stay safe, costs you on average.

The record of people who have been scored this way is sobering. Psychologists reviewing calibration studies by the early 1980s found that people are usually overconfident, most of all on hard questions. Philip Tetlock then spent about twenty years collecting predictions from several hundred political and economic experts and found that many did little better than simple rules of thumb, and that the most confident and most quoted tended to do worse. His later forecasting tournaments had a more hopeful result: some people are reliably good at this, and what they have in common is less a matter of knowledge than of habit. They think in probabilities, update in small steps and keep score.

That is what this page is for. Most arguments in politics and economics contain a forecast in disguise: this tax will raise that much, this candidate will win, this policy will cut that number. Writing the forecast down with a probability, and coming back to it, is the cheapest test of judgment there is. The log is built early in the year so that by the Forecasting League on Day 314 there is a year of resolved predictions to learn from.

How it works

A prediction

Each entry has a statement that will turn out true or false, the probability p you give to "true", and the date by which the answer will be known. When that date has passed and the entry is still open it is flagged as overdue. Resolving it records the outcome o, which is 1 if it happened and 0 if it did not, and the day you resolved it. "Today" is the date on your device.

The Brier score

score of one forecast = (p − o)²    mean Brier score = (1 / N) Σi (pi − oi)²

Say 70% and it happens: (0.7 − 1)² = 0.09. Say 70% and it does not: (0.7 − 0)² = 0.49. Zero is perfect foresight and 1 is being certain and wrong every time. Someone who says 50% to everything scores (0.5)² = 0.25 whatever happens, so 0.25 is the mark to beat, and the scorecard reports how far below it you are as a percentage: 1 − score / 0.25. Brier's own 1950 formula adds the same term for the outcome that did not occur, which doubles every number; the one-sided form used here is now the usual one for yes-or-no questions.

Why honesty is the best policy

Suppose something really has probability q and you report p. Your expected score is

q(1 − p)² + (1 − q)p² = q(1 − q) + (p − q)²

The first term is fixed by the world. The second is the price of saying something other than the truth, and it is zero only when p = q. This is also where the finding comes from: with q = 0.7 and p = 0.9 the score is 0.21 + 0.04 = 0.25. "Try the scoring rule" under the scorecard evaluates this formula for any pair.

The calibration chart

Resolved forecasts are sorted into probability bins: five of them (0 to 20%, 20 to 40%, and so on, each including its lower end) until there are 60 resolved forecasts, then ten. For each bin the chart plots the average probability you gave against the share of those statements that came true. A dot is drawn larger the more forecasts it holds, and the table under the chart gives the counts. Dots below the diagonal are things that happened less often than you said.

Confidence and results

A forecast of 20% is a forecast of 80% that the statement is false. So every forecast is folded onto the side it leaned to: its confidence is c = max(p, 1 − p), and it counts as a hit if that side came good. Forecasts of exactly 50% lean neither way and are left out. The tile reports the average confidence and the hit rate. To judge whether the gap means anything, the tool asks what would happen if your confidences were exactly right: the number of hits would then have

mean = Σ ci    variance = Σ ci(1 − ci)    z = (hits − mean) / √variance

The tool says "overconfident" only if z is −2 or lower, "underconfident" if it is 2 or higher, and gives no verdict at all with fewer than ten forecasts.

Where the score comes from

Allan Murphy showed in 1973 that the mean Brier score splits into three parts. Group the forecasts by the probability stated. Let group k hold nk forecasts at probability pk, of which a share ōk came true, and let ō be the share of all statements that came true. Then

score = (1/N) Σ nk(pk − ōk)² − (1/N) Σ nk(ōk − ō)² + ō(1 − ō)

The first term is calibration error: are your 70%s really 70%? The second, which is subtracted, is resolution: do you say high numbers for things that happen and low numbers for things that do not? The third is the uncertainty in the questions themselves and has nothing to do with you. Because each group shares one probability the identity is exact, and a test checks it on random logs.

Editing, and what counts

An open prediction can be edited. If you change its probability the log stamps the date, and the score uses the latest number. A resolved prediction cannot be edited until you undo the resolution. Deleting asks first. The tag filter narrows both the lists and the scorecard, so you can see your calibration on one topic.

Where the log lives

Your log is stored in this browser's local storage, on this device, under the key 365:day24:log. It is not sent to any server, and this page makes no network requests. That also means it is gone if you clear the browser's site data, and it does not follow you to another device. "Export JSON" writes the whole log to a file. "Export CSV" writes one row per prediction with the columns id, statement, probability_percent, created, due, tags, outcome, resolved and revised, which opens in any spreadsheet. "Import a file" reads either format and adds what is new: a prediction with an id already in the log, or with the same statement, creation date and due date, is skipped, and rows that cannot be read are reported and left out.

The example log is generated in memory, with its dates placed around today, and is never written to storage or to a file: the export buttons always write your own log, even while the example is on screen. Adding a prediction while it is showing starts your own log with that one prediction in it and nothing from the example.

What this cannot tell you

  • Much, from a few forecasts. A mean of twenty squared errors is a noisy number. One miss at 95% costs 0.9025, more than twenty-two hits at 80% put together. The luck test is rough as well: it treats your forecasts as independent, and predictions about the same election or the same exam are not.
  • How you compare with anyone else. The score depends on the questions. Predict that the sun will rise and you score a perfect 0. Two people's Brier scores can only be compared on the same questions, which is what the Forecasting League on Day 314 is for.
  • Whether you were honest with yourself. You write the statement, you decide whether it happened, and you can edit or delete. A vague statement such as "the economy does badly" can be resolved to taste. The log cannot stop that. Statements with a checkable test and a date can.
  • What you thought at the start. The score uses your latest probability. Revise a forecast the day before the answer is known and the log will flatter your original judgment. Forecasting tournaments score every day's forecast for this reason; this log does not.
  • How good your judgment is, from calibration alone. Someone who gives the long-run base rate for everything is perfectly calibrated and tells you nothing. Calibration needs resolution beside it. The Brier score is also gentle on extreme errors about rare events: saying 1% for something that happens costs 0.98 and saying 10% costs 0.81, though the first belief is far worse.
  • Anything that is not yes or no. There is no place here for "inflation will be between 2 and 3%" or for a choice among several candidates, except by turning it into a statement that is true or false.

Improvements and extensions

  • Keep every revision of a probability with its date and score the average over the life of the question, as tournaments do, so that late updates earn less.
  • Report the logarithmic score beside the Brier score, since it punishes confident misses far harder.
  • Put intervals on the picture: a bootstrap interval for the mean score and a binomial interval on each calibration dot.
  • Export the due dates as a calendar file so that the day a prediction resolves is in your diary.
  • Allow forecasts over several outcomes or over ranges of a number, scored with the multi-category Brier score.
  • Offer shared question sets with fixed resolution rules, so that scores from different people mean the same thing. That is the plan for Day 314.
