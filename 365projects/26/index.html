Nature versus Nurture Simulator

Build a population in which you decide how much genes and environments vary. Then run a twin study on it and see what the heritability estimate does, and does not, tell you.

One finding

With genetic variance held at 50, heritability is 0.50 when environmental variance is also 50, 0.91 when environments are made nearly equal, and 0.20 when they are made very unequal. The genes never changed. A simulated study of 500 identical and 500 fraternal twin pairs puts the first figure at 0.57, with a 95% interval from 0.40 to 0.73.

The idea

Francis Galton set "nature and nurture" side by side in the 1870s and suggested that twins could be used to tell them apart. The question most people then want to ask is how much of a trait is due to genes and how much to environment. For one person the question has no answer. Someone's height is not four parts genes and one part food, any more than the area of a rectangle is four parts length and one part width: both are needed for there to be any height at all. What can be asked is a question about differences. Across a population, how much of the variation in a trait goes with variation in genes? That share is called heritability. The idea behind it comes from R. A. Fisher's 1918 paper, which introduced the word variance and the practice of dividing it into parts with different causes.

The classic way to estimate it uses two kinds of twins. Identical twins have the same genes. Fraternal twins share, on average, half of the genetic differences that vary between people. If both kinds share their family surroundings to the same degree, then whatever makes identical twins more alike than fraternal twins must be genetic, and doubling the gap between the two correlations gives the heritability. A large 2015 review of fifty years of such studies put the average across human traits at about a half.

The number is real, and it is narrow, and it is misread in at least four ways. It is not a property of a trait but of a population in a particular range of environments: make environments more alike and heritability rises, make them more unequal and it falls, with no gene altered. It describes the spread across people, not the make-up of any one of them. It says nothing about why two groups differ on average, which is Richard Lewontin's point about two handfuls of seed from one sack, one raised on a rich diet and one on a poor one (told on this page as two fields): inside each batch the differences between plants can be almost wholly genetic while the difference between the batches is wholly the feeding. And it is not a measure of how fixed a trait is. Phenylketonuria is caused by a single gene, and a diet started in infancy prevents most of the harm. Height is among the most heritable of human traits, yet average heights in many countries rose by several centimetres over the twentieth century as food and health improved.

These points matter outside the classroom because heritability figures are quoted in arguments about schooling, health, inequality and differences between groups, often as if they settled what can be changed or who is responsible. They do not, in either direction: a high figure does not show that a gap between groups is genetic, and it does not show that it is environmental. This page takes no position on any real trait or any real group. It builds made-up populations where the truth is known, so that what a heritability estimate can and cannot recover is visible.

How it works

The population

Each simulated person has a trait value made of an average and four pushes above or below it:

P = 100 + G + C + E + I

G is the push from the person's genes, with variance VA. C is the push from the environment a family shares, with variance VC. E is everything unique to the person, including chance, with variance VE. All are drawn from normal distributions. Writing g and c for the genetic and family-environment values scaled to variance 1, the interaction term is

I = √VI · (g c − ρ)

so that the effect of a person's genes is larger in some family environments than in others. The last setting, ρ, is the correlation between g and c: how far family environments follow the parents' genes, as when parents pass on both.

Families are built to respect how inheritance works. Each family has a mid-parent genetic value m with variance one half. Each child's g is m plus an independent draw with variance one half, which makes the correlation between siblings one half. Identical twins receive the same draw; fraternal twins receive separate ones. The family environment is c = 2ρm + √(1 − 2ρ²) z with z a fresh standard normal, which gives c variance 1 and correlation ρ with every child's g. This construction is why ρ stops at 0.7.

The true decomposition

Because the page made the population, it knows where the variance comes from without estimating anything:

VP = VA + VC + VE
    + VI(1 + ρ²) + 2ρ√(VAVC)

h² = VA / VP

The fourth term is the variance of the interaction and the fifth is twice the covariance of genes and family environment. Neither is "genetic" or "environmental": the bar in the tool shows them as their own segments. With both set to zero the bar has three parts and heritability is the first.

The twin study

The simulated researcher sees only trait values for n identical and n fraternal pairs. For each kind the page computes the intraclass correlation, which treats the two twins of a pair symmetrically:

r = Σ (x1 − x̄)(x2 − x̄)
    ÷ ( ½ Σ [ (x1 − x̄)² + (x2 − x̄)² ] )

with x̄ the mean of all twins of that kind. In the plain model identical twins share G and C, so their expected correlation is (VA + VC)/VP, and fraternal twins share half of the genetic variance, giving (½VA + VC)/VP. Falconer's formulas follow by subtraction:

h² = 2(rMZ − rDZ)
c² = 2rDZ − rMZ
e² = 1 − rMZ

The interval shown is the estimate plus or minus 1.96 standard errors, taking each correlation to have variance (1 − r²)²/n, so the standard error of h² is 2√[ ((1 − rMZ²)² + (1 − rDZ²)²) / n ]. A test compares this with the spread of the estimate across 400 simulated studies. The interval is a normal approximation, so in a small study it can run below 0 or above 1. The doubling is what makes twin estimates noisy: with 500 pairs of each kind the interval is about 0.17 either side.

What interaction and correlation do to the estimate

In the full model identical twins share everything except E, and fraternal twins share half of VA, all of VC, all of the covariance, and VI(½ + ρ²) of the interaction variance VI(1 + ρ²):

rMZ = (VP − VE) / VP
rDZ = ( ½VA + VC + 2ρ√(VAVC)
    + VI(½ + ρ²) ) / VP

Working the same subtractions through gives what the twin estimates settle on as the study grows:

2(rMZ − rDZ) → (VA + VI) / VP
2rDZ − rMZ → ( VC + 2ρ√(VAVC)
    + ρ²VI ) / VP

Identical twins share the interaction completely and fraternal twins share half of it when ρ is zero, which is the pattern genes themselves show, so the twin method counts interaction with the shared environment as genetic. The covariance term is shared equally by both kinds of twin and drops out of the first subtraction, to reappear in the estimate of shared environment, and when ρ is above zero a part ρ²VI of the interaction goes there with it. This agrees with the standard account of the twin design: in Purcell's summary, interaction between genes and shared environment "acts like" genes and correlation between them "acts like" shared environment. The tool states both mismatches when they occur, and the tests check the algebra against simulated studies of 50,000 pairs.

The two fields

Two thousand plants grow in each field. A plant's height is 100 plus a genetic value with variance h² × 100 plus a soil-and-chance value with variance (1 − h²) × 100, and plants in the poor field are shorter on average by the chosen gap. The last slider decides how the gap is caused: a share s of it is subtracted from the genetic values of the poor field's seed and the rest from its environment. The heights are the same numbers whatever s is, since only the sum is ever observed, and so are the heritabilities inside each field. To keep chance out of the comparison, the plants in the poor field are given the same genetic values as the plants in the rich field before the shift, so the genetic part of the gap is exactly s times the gap. With s at zero this is Lewontin's example, with fields standing in for his two batches of seed raised on different nutrients. In his version every plant in a batch was fed alike, which is the slider at 1.00.

What this cannot tell you

  • Anything about a real trait. Every variance here is a slider. The page shows what follows from a set of assumptions, not what is true of height, health, schooling or anything else in people.
  • How much of one person's trait is genetic. Heritability is a ratio of variances across a population. The table of five people shows why it does not carry over: in one person the pushes can cancel, and with interaction there is no way to split them at all.
  • Why two groups differ. Heritability inside groups is compatible with a gap between them that is entirely environmental, entirely genetic, or any mixture. The two-field panel produces identical data in all three cases. It is a model of plants, in which the cause of the gap is a slider, and it is not evidence about any difference between groups of people.
  • Whether a trait can be changed. The figure describes the environments that happened to exist in the population measured. It is silent about environments nobody in it experienced, such as a new diet, treatment or school.
  • Whether real twin studies meet their assumptions. Here identical and fraternal twins share their surroundings equally, parents pair at random and genetic effects add up, because the simulation was written that way. In real data each of these can fail and bias the estimate, and none can be checked from twin correlations alone.
  • What the environment is. "Shared" and "non-shared" are labels for variance, not causes. The non-shared part includes measurement error and chance in development. A variance component does not name anything that could be acted on.

Improvements and extensions

  • Simulate actual genes: many locations with two variants each, with dominance and interaction between them, so that broad and narrow heritability come apart.
  • Let parents pair by similarity, and let identical twins share more of their surroundings than fraternal twins, to show the size and direction of the bias each causes.
  • Add adoption and wider family designs, and fit the full variance-components model by maximum likelihood in place of Falconer's subtraction.
  • Draw reaction norms, the trait each genetic value produces across a range of environments, which is the picture Lewontin argued should replace a single percentage.
  • Add interaction with the non-shared environment, which the twin method files under non-shared environment, alongside the kind modelled here.
  • Add a cross-sowing experiment to the two fields, so the visitor can do the one thing that reveals where the gap comes from.
