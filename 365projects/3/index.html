Monty Hall Monte Carlo

Three doors, one car, and a host who opens a door you did not pick. Play it yourself, then let the computer play 100,000 games and watch the odds settle.

One finding

In 100,000 simulated games (seed 3), switching won 66,892 times and staying 33,108: 66.9% against 33.1%. Let the host open a door at random instead and, in the 66,664 games where he happened not to reveal the car, switching won 33,263 and staying 33,401. The advantage of switching is gone, because it was the host's knowledge that carried the information.

The idea

You pick one of three doors. The host, who knows where the car is, opens one of the other two to show a goat, and offers you the chance to switch. Two doors are left, so it feels like an even bet, and most people stay. They are wrong: switching wins two times in three.

The puzzle was posed by the statistician Steve Selvin in 1975 and became famous in 1990, when Marilyn vos Savant gave the correct answer in her Parade column. By her own estimate she received about 10,000 letters, most of them telling her she had made a mistake, and close to 1,000 of the critical ones were signed by people with doctorates. It is a reliable demonstration that intelligent people reason badly about conditional probability, and that a simulation can persuade where an argument does not.

What makes it a problem about rational choice under uncertainty is the question of what counts as evidence. The open door looks like it tells you nothing: you already knew one of the other doors hid a goat. But the host's choice was constrained by where the car is, and a constrained action leaks information. The second setting on this page makes the point by removing the constraint: a host who opens a door at random, and merely happens to show a goat, tells you nothing useful, and the odds really are even.

How it works

Counting the cases

Your first pick is right one time in three. If it is right, switching loses. If it is wrong (two times in three), the car is behind one of the other two doors, the host must open the one with the goat, and switching wins for certain. So

P(stay wins) = 1/3      P(switch wins) = 2/3

The same result from Bayes' theorem

Say you pick door 1 and the host opens door 2. If the car is behind door 1, he could have opened 2 or 3, and opens 2 half the time. If it is behind door 3, he had to open 2. If it is behind door 2, he could not have opened it. Each door starts with probability 1/3, so

P(car at 3 | host opens 2) = (1 · ⅓) / (½ · ⅓ + 0 · ⅓ + 1 · ⅓) = 2/3

For the host who opens at random, the last term changes: with the car behind door 3 he opens door 2 only half the time, the same as when it is behind door 1. (With the car behind door 2 he may well open it, but then you see the car, not a goat, so that case still counts for nothing.) The two likelihoods are equal, so the evidence favours neither door and the answer is 1/2.

More doors

With N doors, of which a knowing host opens m, staying still wins with probability 1/N. The other (N − 1)/N is shared equally among the N − 1 − m doors he left closed:

P(switch wins) = (N − 1) / (N · (N − 1 − m))

That is the chance of winning by switching to one of those doors, chosen at random as the simulation does. Set ten doors with eight opened and switching wins nine times in ten, which is the version where the logic is easiest to see: the host has walked past the car's door on purpose.

A host who opens m of the other doors at random shows the car in m/N of games. In the rest, nothing distinguishes the N − m doors still closed, so staying and switching each win with probability 1/(N − m).

The simulation

Each simulated game places the car at random, makes a random first pick, has the host open doors according to the rule you chose, and then scores the game twice: once for staying and once for switching to a random closed door. The random numbers come from a seeded generator (mulberry32), so the same seed replays the same 100,000 games.

By the law of large numbers the share of wins approaches the true probability. How close it gets is given by the standard error: for a true probability p after n games, about 95% of runs land within

± 1.96 · √(p(1 − p) / n)

With p = 2/3 that is ± 9 percentage points after 100 games, ± 0.9 after 10,000 and ± 0.3 after 100,000. This is the margin printed beside each theoretical value under the buttons. The chart uses a log scale for the number of games so the early wobble and the late calm are both visible.

What this cannot tell you

  • What to do on a real game show. The 2/3 answer needs a host who knows where the car is, always opens a goat door and always offers the switch. A host free to offer the switch only when your first pick was right would make switching a certain loss. The rules are doing the work, and the puzzle as usually told leaves them unstated.
  • Why the answer is 2/3. A simulation shows that something is so. The reason is in the counting argument above. 100,000 games are evidence, not a proof.
  • Much from your own games. Ten games by hand have a margin of error of about 30 percentage points. Staying and winning twice running happens one time in nine.
  • Why people stay. In Granberg and Brown's experiments only 12% of people switched the first time they met the problem, and people said they would feel more frustrated and angry if they switched and lost. Whether the pull is regret, attachment to a first choice or plain miscounting is a question for the behavioural experiments from Day 146 onwards, not for a simulation.
  • Anything about hosts with preferences. If the host has a favourite door when he has a choice, which door he opens carries extra information and the odds for a particular game move away from 2/3. This page assumes he chooses evenly.

Improvements and extensions

  • Add hosts with other rules: one who prefers a particular door, and one who offers the switch only when it suits him, to show how the answer follows the protocol.
  • Draw the probability tree next to the game, lighting up the branch you are on.
  • Show the Three Prisoners problem alongside it: the same structure, told as a story about a jailer.
  • Record how often visitors switch on their first game compared with their tenth, kept in the browser, to see whether playing teaches what the argument does not.
  • Let the visitor write their own strategy as a rule and score it against the two built in.
