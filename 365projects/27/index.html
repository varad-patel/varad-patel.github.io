Raven Paradox Evidence Meter

Does a white shoe support the claim that all ravens are black? Logic says it should. Pick what turned up and how it was found, and the meter shows how much it confirms the claim: a little, a hundred-thousandth, or nothing at all.

One finding

In a world of 100 ravens and a million non-black other things, weighed against a rival in which 10% of ravens are not black, a black raven picked from the ravens is 1.111 times as likely if all ravens are black, and a white shoe picked from the non-black things is 1.00001 times as likely. Both confirm, and it takes 10,536 such shoes to match one raven. A white shoe that merely turns up when sampling everything is worth exactly nothing on this page's opening assumption, that the rival world differs only in the colour of its ravens. On the textbook assumption, that both worlds hold the same number of non-black things, the same shoe is worth 1.00001 however it was found.

The idea

Carl Hempel raised the puzzle in 1937 and set it out in full in 1945, from two principles that each look harmless. The first, due to Jean Nicod, says a generalisation is confirmed by its instances: "all ravens are black" is supported by finding a raven that is black. The second says that two statements which mean the same thing are supported by the same evidence. Now "all ravens are black" means exactly the same as "everything that is not black is not a raven". An instance of the second statement is a thing that is not black and not a raven, such as a white shoe. So a white shoe confirms that all ravens are black, and you can do ornithology without leaving the house.

Hempel accepted the conclusion and blamed the sense of absurdity on background knowledge that cannot be switched off. The answer that has become standard is older than his best-known paper and comes from Janina Hosiasson-Lindenbaum in 1940: treat confirmation as a matter of degree. Evidence supports a hypothesis to the extent that it is more probable if the hypothesis is true than if it is false. A white shoe does then confirm that all ravens are black, by an amount so small that intuition rounds it to zero, because the world holds so many more non-black things than ravens.

Two refinements matter as much as the headline. I. J. Good showed in 1967 that even a black raven need not confirm: if the alternative to "all ravens are black" is a world with far more ravens in it, meeting a black raven is a sign you are in that world. And the way the object was found changes its weight. Going through the non-black things looking for a raven is a test the hypothesis could have failed. Looking down at your own shoe is not. The tool keeps these apart: you choose the object, the group it was drawn from, and what the rival world is like.

One warning for readers who know the standard answer. The textbook version assumes that the rival world, the one in which some ravens are not black, contains the same number of non-black things as the world in which all are black. On that assumption a white shoe confirms by the same minute amount however it is found. This page opens on a different and simpler rival, in which the same objects exist and some ravens are a different colour. There a white shoe confirms only if it was drawn from the non-black things, and one that merely turns up is no evidence at all. Both are legitimate models and the tool offers both. The assumption that separates them is the one Peter Vranas argued the standard solution relies on without defending.

The lesson reaches well beyond birds. A claim is not supported by the bare number of cases that fit it. What counts is how much less likely each case would have been had the claim been false, and that depends on where the cases came from. A policy "confirmed" by a hundred success stories that would have happened anyway has collected a hundred white shoes.

How it works

Two worlds

Every object is a raven or not, and black or not, so a world is four counts. Write R for the number of ravens, K for the black things that are not ravens and W for the non-black things that are not ravens. The hypothesis H, "all ravens are black", is the world

black ravens = R   non-black ravens = 0
black others = K   non-black others = W

The rival says a share f of ravens are not black. That alone does not fix a world, so the tool offers three ways to complete it, each listed as black ravens, non-black ravens, black others, non-black others.

Only the ravens' colours differ. The same objects, with fR of the ravens repainted:

(1 − f)R    fR    K    W

Same number of non-black things. The assumption most textbook treatments make: the truth of H is independent of how many things are ravens and of how many are non-black. Each non-black raven then takes the place of a non-black other thing, which becomes black:

(1 − f)R    fR    K + fR    W − fR

A different number of ravens. Good's case: the rival world has R′ ravens, with the other things unchanged:

(1 − f)R′    fR′    K    W

An observation is a draw from a pool

An observation is one object, drawn uniformly at random from a stated group, whose other property is then seen. The five groups are the ravens, the non-black things, the black things, the non-ravens and everything. The probability of drawing a given kind of object is its share of the pool, in whichever world is the true one:

P(E | world) =
  number of objects of that kind
  ÷ number of objects in the pool

The pool is counted in the same world as the object. That is where the sampling procedure enters: the pool "non-black things" has W members if all ravens are black and W + fR if they are not.

The likelihood ratio

The weight of an observation is the ratio of its two probabilities:

LR = P(E | H) ÷ P(E | rival)

Above 1 it confirms H, below 1 it counts against, and exactly 1 is no evidence. Working through the cases with only the ravens' colours differing, which is the assumption the tool opens on and not the textbook one:

  • A black raven from the ravens. Certain under H; probability 1 − f under the rival. LR = 1 ÷ (1 − f). With f = 10% that is 1.111.
  • A white shoe from the non-black things. Certain under H, since every non-black thing is a non-raven; probability W ÷ (W + fR) under the rival. LR = 1 + fR ÷ W. With 100 ravens and a million non-black things that is 1.00001.
  • A white shoe from everything. Probability W ÷ (R + K + W) in both worlds, because the shoes and the total are the same in both. LR = 1.
  • A raven that is not black. Impossible under H, possible under the rival. LR = 0, from any pool that contains it.

On the textbook assumption every pool has the same size in both worlds, so the way of picking drops out and a white shoe has LR = W ÷ (W − fR) however it was found. That is the usual statement of the Bayesian resolution. The same assumption makes a black shoe count slightly against H, with LR = K ÷ (K + fR).

From weight to belief

If your confidence in H before looking is p, Bayes' theorem gives your confidence afterwards. In odds it is one multiplication:

P(H | E) = p · P(E | H)
    ÷ ( p · P(E | H) + (1 − p) · P(E | rival) )
odds after = odds before × LR

Five measures

Philosophers have proposed several ways to turn "how much does E confirm H" into a number. The table in the tool shows five:

  • the difference P(H | E) − P(H), in percentage points;
  • the ratio P(H | E) ÷ P(H);
  • the likelihood ratio above;
  • its logarithm to base 2, the weight of evidence in bits, which adds up across independent observations;
  • the Kemeny-Oppenheim measure (P(E | H) − P(E | rival)) ÷ (P(E | H) + P(E | rival)), which runs from −1 for a refutation to +1 for a proof and is what the meter shows.

The first two change with your starting confidence. The last three do not. All five always agree on the sign, and in the opening world all five put the white shoe about four orders of magnitude below the raven.

The two comparison tiles

Because log-likelihood ratios add, the number of observations of one kind that weigh as much as one black raven picked from the ravens is ln LRraven ÷ ln LRthis. For the opening world that is ln 1.1111 ÷ ln 1.00001 = 10,536. The number of independent observations needed to take your confidence from p to 99% is the smallest whole number n with

n · ln LR ≥ ln(0.99 ÷ 0.01) − ln(p ÷ (1 − p))

From 50% that is 44 black ravens or 459,515 white shoes. The chart draws the same thing as a curve: confidence after n observations is the starting odds times LRn, turned back into a probability.

Small numbers

Much of the interest is in the sixth decimal place and beyond, where subtracting two nearly equal probabilities would lose most of the digits. The tool works with ln LR throughout and computes each logarithm of a ratio a ÷ b as log1p((a − b) ÷ b), which stays accurate when a and b are almost equal. Counts are limited to a billion so that this holds.

What this cannot tell you

  • How much a real white shoe confirms. The answer depends on how many non-black things there are, and the world has no agreed inventory of "things". Count shoes, or shoelaces, or grains of sand, and the number changes by orders of magnitude. The model shows why the amount is minute. It cannot say what the amount is.
  • What the alternative to "all ravens are black" is. The tool weighs the hypothesis against one rival with a fixed share of non-black ravens. A real doubter is unsure about that share, and a spread of rivals gives different numbers from any single one.
  • Which rival world is the right one. The three assumptions give different answers for the same white shoe: nothing, a hundred-thousandth, or more. The textbook assumption is convenient because it makes the sampling method irrelevant, and Peter Vranas has argued that the standard solution never justifies it. The tool can show that the choice matters. It cannot make it.
  • How the object was really found. Each procedure here is a uniform random draw from a well-defined group. Nobody meets ravens that way. Birds seen near a house are not a random sample of ravens, and a black bird is easier to recognise as a raven than a white one.
  • What happens when a small world runs out. Repeated observations are treated as independent, as if each object were put back. In an aviary of 20 ravens, looking at all 20 would settle the question, and the chart does not show that.
  • Whether confirmation is a matter of probability at all. The measures here are Bayesian. Hempel's own theory was not, Popper denied that hypotheses are confirmed by instances in the first place, and even among Bayesians the five measures can rank pairs of observations differently in other problems.

Improvements and extensions

  • Replace the single rival with a prior over the share of non-black ravens and average over it, which connects this page to the rule of succession on day 30.
  • Sample without replacement, so that a small world can be exhausted and the hypothesis proved by checking every raven.
  • Let the visitor collect a mixed bag of observations and watch the weights add up.
  • Model biased sampling, such as non-black ravens that are less likely to be noticed or to be recorded as ravens.
  • Add further confirmation measures and build a case where they disagree about which of two observations confirms more.
  • Add Goodman's "grue" as a second paradox, where the same instances appear to confirm two generalisations that contradict each other.
