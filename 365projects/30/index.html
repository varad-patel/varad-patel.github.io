Sunrise and Induction Calculator

The sun has risen on every day on record. What is the chance it rises tomorrow? Enter how often something has happened and how often it has not, and compare what Laplace's rule and four other starting assumptions say about next time.

One finding

After 1,826,213 sunrises in a row, Laplace's rule puts the odds of another at 1,826,214 to 1, Jeffreys' prior at 3,652,427 to 1 and a sceptical prior at 182,621 to 1. The three probabilities lie within 0.0006 percentage points of each other, yet they differ twentyfold on the chance of failure, and under Laplace's rule it is only an even bet that the run lasts another 1,826,214 days.

The idea

David Hume asked what entitles anyone to believe that the sun will rise tomorrow. Not logic: "the sun will not rise" contradicts nothing. Not experience either, he argued, because every inference from experience already assumes that the future will resemble the past, and that assumption is the very thing in question. To defend it by pointing out that it has worked so far is to argue in a circle. This is the problem of induction, and nearly three centuries on it has been sharpened and worked around but not dissolved.

Pierre-Simon Laplace gave the most famous reply in numbers. Suppose an event has a fixed but unknown chance of happening, and suppose that before seeing anything you thought every value of that chance equally likely. If the event then happens s times in n trials, the probability that it happens on the next is (s + 1) ÷ (n + 2). This is the rule of succession. Laplace applied it to the sunrise: taking recorded history as 5,000 years, or 1,826,213 days, with a sunrise on each, he made the odds of a sunrise tomorrow 1,826,214 to 1. He added at once that the figure is far too low for anyone who understands why the sun rises, a caveat that is quoted much less often than the number.

The calculation does not answer Hume, and the honest way to read it is as a precise statement of what he doubted. It assumes that the trials are exchangeable: that tomorrow is one more draw from the same process as every day before, so that only the count of sunrises matters and not their order. That is the uniformity of nature written as a probability model. Grant it and the arithmetic follows. Withhold it, as Hume does, and the arithmetic has nothing to stand on. Bertrand Russell's chicken, fed by the same hand every morning of its life until the morning the hand wrings its neck, had an excellent track record and the wrong model.

There is a second assumption, and it is the one this page lets you vary. "Knowing nothing beforehand" can be written down in more than one way. Laplace's uniform prior, Harold Jeffreys' prior and J. B. S. Haldane's prior have each been defended as the neutral choice, and they give different answers. With a long mixed record the difference fades. With a short record, or with a record that has no failures in it, it does not. The same question arises every time someone says a system has never failed, a fund has never lost money or a bridge has stood for a century.

How it works

The model

The event has an unknown underlying chance p. Each trial is a success with probability p, independently of the others given p. A record of s successes and f failures, n = s + f trials in all, then has probability proportional to ps(1 − p)f, whatever the order.

Prior and posterior

Belief about p before the record is a Beta distribution with two parameters, a and b:

prior density ∝ pa − 1(1 − p)b − 1

Multiplying by the probability of the record gives a density of the same form, which is why this family is used:

posterior = Beta(a + s, b + f)

So a and b behave like successes and failures seen before the record began. The tool's priors are the uniform Beta(1, 1), Jeffreys' Beta(½, ½), Haldane's Beta(0, 0), a sceptical Beta(1, 10) that starts as if ten failures had been seen, and one you set yourself.

The chance it happens next time

The probability of a success on the next trial is the average of p over the posterior, which for a Beta distribution is

P(next) = (a + s) ÷ (a + b + n)

With a = b = 1 this is Laplace's (s + 1) ÷ (n + 2). The odds in favour are (a + s) ÷ (b + f), which for the sunrise under the uniform prior is (1 + 1,826,213) ÷ 1.

Haldane's prior

Beta(0, 0) is the limit of ever weaker priors and is not a probability distribution: its density, 1 ÷ (p(1 − p)), has infinite area. The posterior Beta(s, f) becomes a real distribution only once the record holds at least one success and one failure. Before that the tool reports the limiting value of the formula, s ÷ n, which is 100% after any unbroken run and 0 ÷ 0 with no data, and it shows no interval and no curve. This is the prior that takes a single sunrise as proof that the sun always rises.

The credible interval

The interval is the range that holds 95% of the posterior. Its ends are quantiles of the Beta distribution, found by bisection on the regularised incomplete beta function, which the tool evaluates with a continued fraction. Normally 2.5% is left out at each end. When the posterior density is highest at 0% or at 100%, as it is after an unbroken run, an equal-tailed interval would leave out the most probable values, so the tool gives the one-sided interval that includes that end. For an unbroken run of n under the uniform prior the interval runs from 0.051 ÷ (n + 1) to 100%. For an event that has never happened in n trials it runs from 0% up to 1 − 0.051 ÷ (n + 1), which is close to the "rule of three", 3 ÷ n.

A run of successes

Write A = a + s and B = b + f. Each success raises the chance of the next, so the probability that the next m trials all succeed is a product of m different terms:

P(m in a row) = A ÷ (A + B) × (A + 1) ÷ (A + B + 1)
    × … × (A + m − 1) ÷ (A + B + m − 1)

The tool computes it from log-gamma functions so that runs of a billion are no harder than runs of ten. Under Laplace's rule after an unbroken run it simplifies to (n + 1) ÷ (n + m + 1), which reaches one half at m = n + 1: a run is an even bet to last about as long again as it already has. The last tile reports that length for the featured prior. The product falls towards zero as m grows, so no finite record makes "it will always happen" probable under any of these priors.

The charts

The first chart draws the posterior density for every prior that gives a proper one, on an axis that runs from the lowest 0.1% point to the highest 99.9% point among them. For the sunrise that is a window about a thousandth of a percentage point wide, just below 100%. The second chart recomputes P(next) for records running from 1 observation to at least ten times the length of yours, keeping your success rate fixed. Its other view shows the chance of failure, (b + failures) ÷ (a + b + observations), on a log scale. After an unbroken run of k that is b ÷ (a + b + k), so two priors' answers stay in the ratio of their bs however large k becomes. That is why the lines run parallel and never meet.

What this cannot tell you

  • Whether the future will resemble the past. Every number here assumes it does, in the exact sense that the next trial is exchangeable with the ones on record. That is Hume's question, and the calculation takes one side of it as a premise.
  • Which prior is right. There is no neutral starting point. After 1,826,213 sunrises the priors on this page still disagree about the chance of failure by a factor of twenty, and Haldane's says there is none. More data of the same kind will never settle it.
  • Anything about causes. The model counts mornings. It knows nothing of orbits, and Laplace said as much. Knowledge of the mechanism can make the true chance far higher than the rule says, or lower: astrophysics expects the sunrises to stop one day, which no count of them could reveal.
  • What counts as a trial. The answer depends on where the record starts and how it is divided. Count 5,000 years as years and not days and the odds fall to 5,001 to 1. Apply the rule to birthdays and a ten-year-old has an 11 in 12 chance of living another year while an eighty-year-old has 81 in 82.
  • What to expect when conditions drift. Machines wear out, people learn, climates change. A record made under old conditions is weaker evidence than its length suggests, and the rule has no way to know.
  • Whether a general law is true. The chance of an endless run is zero under every prior here that is a proper distribution, which leaves only Haldane's. If "the sun always rises" can be supported by evidence, it is not by this method. Day 27 looks at how instances confirm a generalisation.

Improvements and extensions

  • Allow a prior that puts a lump of probability on "it always happens", so that a general law can gain credibility from an unbroken run.
  • Give older observations less weight, with a setting for how fast the past is forgotten, to model a world that drifts.
  • Let the visitor paste a sequence of outcomes and test it for streaks and trends before the rule is applied, since those are signs that the trials are not exchangeable.
  • Extend from two outcomes to several with a Dirichlet prior, the setting of Carnap's continuum of inductive methods.
  • Add a change-point model that asks whether the underlying chance moved part of the way through the record.
  • Add Goodman's "grue": two descriptions of the same record that the rule turns into opposite predictions.
