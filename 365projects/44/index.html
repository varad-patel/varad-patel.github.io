Tax Systems Compared

Enter a household and see what it pays under a flat tax, a progressive schedule and a consumption tax. India's old and new income-tax regimes for 2026-27 are worked out in full beside them.

One finding

Press the ₹12.75 lakh example and then the ₹13 lakh one, leaving the salary box ticked. Under India's new regime for 2026-27 the first household pays no income tax and the second pays ₹26,000, more than its ₹25,000 of extra pay. For both, the table shows ₹1,040 of tax on the next ₹1,000: a marginal rate of 104%. It holds for every rupee of salary from ₹12,75,000 to ₹13,45,588, because the law's "marginal relief" takes each extra rupee and the 4% cess is charged on top.

The idea

Arguments about tax fairness are usually arguments about a curve: how the share of income taken in tax changes as income rises. Adam Smith's first maxim of taxation was that people should contribute "in proportion to their respective abilities", and almost every position since can be read as a view about what that proportion should be. A tax is proportional if everyone pays the same share of income, progressive if the share rises with income and regressive if it falls. Richard Musgrave and Tun Thin set out ways of measuring progression in 1948, and the simplest turns on the average rate, the tax bill divided by income. The marginal rate, the tax on the next rupee earned, is a different thing: it is what a person weighs when deciding whether more work or a pay rise is worth it. James Mirrlees built the modern theory of income taxation in 1971 on the tension between the two. Fairness pulls average rates up at the top, and each increase in an average rate has to be bought with marginal rates somewhere below.

The three families of tax in this tool sit at different points in that argument. A flat tax has one marginal rate. Its supporters value the simplicity and the equal treatment of every rupee, and with a tax-free allowance it is still progressive in Musgrave and Thin's sense, because the average rate climbs from zero towards the single rate without ever reaching it. A progressive schedule raises the marginal rate in steps, so that the average rate keeps rising across a much wider range of incomes, at the cost of more rules and more reason to rearrange income. A consumption tax such as a sales tax or GST taxes what a household spends and leaves what it saves alone. Because richer households save more of their income, a single-rate consumption tax takes a falling share of income as income rises: regressive in any one year, though closer to proportional over a lifetime in which savings are eventually spent. Whether saving should be exempt at all is one of the oldest disputes in public finance, and Atkinson and Stiglitz showed in 1976 how much the answer depends on what an income tax is already doing.

India's income tax makes the comparison concrete, because it offers two schedules at once. The old regime has higher rates and a long list of deductions for saving, insurance, rent and home loans. The new regime, the default, has lower rates over wider bands and almost no deductions. Which is cheaper for a household depends on one number, the deductions it can claim, and the tool works out where the two cross. It also shows what neither headline rate table reveals: the rebates that make low incomes tax-free are withdrawn in ways that push the marginal rate above 100% for a narrow band of incomes in the new regime, and bring back the entire tax with a single extra rupee in the old one.

How it works

The household

The household is treated as a single resident taxpayer with income Y a year, all of it ordinary income (salary, business or interest). It spends a share c of that income and saves the rest. Under the old regime it claims deductions D that you add up yourself.

Slabs

A progressive schedule is a list of bands. Each rate applies only to the income inside its band, so for bands with lower edges b0 = 0 < b1 < … and rates r0, r1, …

tax on the slabs(T) = Σk rk × max(0, min(T, bk+1) − bk)

"Your own slabs" is exactly this and nothing else. The two Indian regimes add four further steps.

India's income tax, tax year 2026-27

Tax year 2026-27 runs from 1 April 2026 to 31 March 2027 and is the first under the Income-tax Act, 2025. The figures below are enacted law, read on 12 October 2026 in two places. The new-regime slabs, the standard deduction and the rebate are sections 202, 19 and 156 of that Act, in the Income Tax Department's copy "as amended by Finance Act, 2026". The old-regime slabs, the surcharge with its marginal relief and the cess are in the Finance Act, 2026 (No. 4 of 2026), which received the President's assent on 30 March 2026: section 3 and Part I-B of its First Schedule. That Act left the slabs, the rebate and the standard deduction as they were.

  2. Rebate (section 156). Old regime: if T is at most ₹5,00,000 the tax on the slabs is cancelled, up to ₹12,500. New regime: if T is at most ₹12,00,000 it is cancelled, up to ₹60,000. Above ₹12,00,000 the new regime gives marginal relief: the tax may not exceed the income above ₹12,00,000.

new-regime tax after rebate = min( tax on the slabs(T), T − 12,00,000 )   for T > 12,00,000

The two sides are equal where 60,000 + 0.15x = x, that is at x = 60,000 / 0.85 = 70,588 above ₹12,00,000. Up to there the tax rises rupee for rupee with income. The old regime has no such relief.

  1. Surcharge (Finance Act, 2026). 10% of the tax when T is above ₹50 lakh, 15% above ₹1 crore and 25% above ₹2 crore; the old regime alone charges 37% above ₹5 crore. Just above a threshold H there is marginal relief: tax and surcharge together may not exceed what was payable at H by more than T − H.
  2. Cess (Finance Act, 2026, section 3(15)). A health and education cess of 4% is added to tax plus surcharge. No relief applies to the cess, as the memorandum to the Finance Bill says in terms, which is why the marginal rate inside a relief zone is 104% and not 100%.

Flat tax and consumption tax

flat tax = r × max(0, Y − A)

with one rate r and a tax-free amount A. Its average rate is r(1 − A/Y), which rises towards r.

consumption tax = t / (1 + t) × max(0, c Y − E)

Here t is added to prices, as GST is, so of every rupee handed over for a taxed good t/(1 + t) is tax. c Y is what the household spends, tax included, and E is its yearly spending on goods the tax leaves out. The household is assumed to spend the same share of its income whichever tax applies.

Rates across incomes

The table gives, for the household's own income, the average rate (tax divided by income) and the tax on the next ₹1,000 of income, with that tax as a share of the ₹1,000 as the marginal rate. The chart repeats both for 160 incomes spaced evenly on a log scale from ₹2 lakh to ₹100 crore, with extra points placed just below and above every threshold in the two Indian regimes so that no step falls between two points. Deductions and the salary tick stay as entered. For the consumption tax the chart needs a share spent at every income, and uses

c(y) = c − f × log2(y / Y), kept between 20% and 100%

where f is the slider "fall in the share spent for each doubling of income". This is an assumption that you set, not an estimate from household surveys. At f = 0 the consumption tax is exactly proportional to income.

Old regime or new

The old regime's tax can only fall as deductions rise, so the tool finds by bisection the smallest D, to the rupee, at which the old regime costs no more than the new. For a ₹15,00,000 salary that is ₹5,43,750: the new regime charges ₹97,500, and the old regime's bill comes down to that only when its taxable income falls to ₹9,06,250.

What this cannot tell you

  • Your actual tax bill. This is not a return. It leaves out capital gains and other income taxed at special rates, the limits on each deduction, the rules for house rent allowance and home loans, losses carried forward, the alternative minimum tax, non-residents, and the rounding of income and tax to the nearest ten rupees. State taxes such as profession tax are not included.
  • Which system is fairest. The tool shows who pays what. Whether a rising average rate is fair, or how steep it should be, depends on values the tool does not hold, and on what the tax pays for, which is not modelled at all.
  • What would happen if a country switched. The flat, custom and consumption taxes use whatever rates you type and are not set to raise the same revenue as India's income tax. People would also work, save and report income differently under each, and prices and wages would move; here income is fixed.
  • How a real consumption tax falls across households. The share of income spent at other incomes is a slider, not data, and a year's income is a poor guide to a lifetime's. Students, retired people and those in a bad year spend more than they earn.
  • A household with two earners. India taxes individuals. Two people earning ₹7.5 lakh each pay much less than one earning ₹15 lakh, and the tool only does the second.
  • Next year. Slabs, rebates and surcharges are reset by each Finance Act. These are the rules for tax year 2026-27 as they stood on 12 October 2026.

Improvements and extensions

  • Set the flat and consumption rates so that each system raises the same revenue from a realistic distribution of incomes, and then compare who gains and who loses.
  • Split the household into two earners, and add the specific deductions with their legal limits so that the old regime is computed and not typed in.
  • Replace the spending slider with spending shares by income group from a household consumption survey.
  • Add a summary measure of progressivity for each schedule, such as Kakwani's index, computed over an income distribution.
  • Show the lifetime view of a consumption tax beside the one-year view.
  • Add other countries' schedules, and a negative income tax, as further presets.
